# Proposition J — Removal of Foreclosure Exemption for Real Property Transfer Tax

- **Jurisdiction:** City and County of San Francisco
- **Kind:** Real property transfer tax ordinance, placed on the ballot by the Board of Supervisors
- **Who votes:** all registered San Francisco voters (consolidated city and county; one electorate)
- **Vote threshold** (VIP PDF p. 146): "This measure requires 50%+1 affirmative votes to pass."

## Ballot question (p. 146)

> Shall the City permanently apply a transfer tax of between 0.5% and 6% of a property's fair market value, on the transfer of properties such as commercial, industrial, and large residential properties, that are transferred to a lender because of foreclosure or to avoid foreclosure, for estimated annual revenue of $100 million to $150 million?

## Digest by the Ballot Simplification Council (p. 146)

**The Way It Is Now:**
> San Francisco charges a tax when most real estate property in the City changes hands. The amount of the tax depends on the property's sales price or value.
>
> When a lender takes over a property through foreclosure, or when an owner hands over a property to the lender to avoid foreclosure, there is a tax break. The lender pays tax only on the difference between what the property sold for and what is still owed to the lender, including interest and foreclosure costs.
>
> State law limits the total revenue, including tax revenue, the City may spend each year. The voters may approve increases to this limit for up to four years.

**The Proposal:**
> Proposition J would apply a transfer tax for properties such as commercial, industrial, and large residential real estate that a lender takes through foreclosure or is handed over to the lender to avoid foreclosure. Starting March 1, 2027, these properties would be taxed on their full market value.
>
> The tax break would still apply to the transfer of:
> 1. single-family residences,
> 2. multi-family residential buildings with fewer than five units, and
> 3. mixed-use properties with fewer than five residential units and only one floor of non-residential space.
>
> Proposition J would increase the City's spending limit for four years.

**A "YES" Vote Means:**
> If you vote "yes," you want to apply a transfer tax for properties such as commercial, industrial, and large residential properties, that are transferred to a lender because of foreclosure.

**A "NO" Vote Means:**
> If you vote "no," you do not want to make these changes.

## Controller's Statement on "J" (pp. 146–147)

> City Controller Greg Wagner has issued the following statement on the fiscal impact of Proposition J:
> Should the proposed initiative ordinance be approved by the voters, in my opinion, it would significantly increase the City's Real Property Transfer Tax revenues although the amount generated each year would be unpredictable and likely to change significantly year-to-year. The measure could bring an average of $100 million to $150 million annually over the first five years. (p. 146)

Rates and dollar figures:
> Currently the City partially exempts transfers of real property to a lender by foreclosure or in lieu of foreclosure from its Real Property Transfer Tax, which imposes a tax ranging from 0.5% to 6% of properties' consideration or value when they are transferred. (p. 146)

> For context, in Fiscal Year (FY) 2021-2022, before the City's real estate market declined due to the pandemic, the proposed ordinance would have generated $3.2 million. However, several years later in FY 2025-26, the proposed ordinance would have generated $232.5 million. (p. 147)

> There may be years where the proposed measure generates next to no new tax revenue. (p. 147)

Oddity, as printed: the Controller calls Prop J a "proposed initiative ordinance", but "How "J" Got on the Ballot" says the Board of Supervisors placed it.

## How "J" Got on the Ballot (p. 147)

> On July 28, 2026, the Board of Supervisors voted 10 to 0, with one Supervisor excused, to place Proposition J on the ballot. The Supervisors voted as follows:

## Arguments (pp. 148–149)

Every argument page carries: "Arguments are the opinions of the authors and have not been checked for accuracy by any official agency." and "Arguments are printed as submitted. Spelling and grammatical errors have not been corrected."

**Proponent's Argument** (p. 148). No separate signer line is printed. The argument says it was "Written by Supervisor Bilal Mahmood and Mayor Daniel Lurie" and ends with "PROPOSITION J is endorsed by:" Mayor Daniel Lurie; State Senator Scott Wiener; Assemblymember Matt Haney; Assemblymember Catherine Stefani; SEIU 1021; SEIU 2015; Teamsters 665; Board President Rafael Mandelman; Supervisor Bilal Mahmood; Supervisor Myrna Melgar; Supervisor Danny Sauter; Supervisor Alan Wong; Supervisor Matt Dorsey; Supervisor Chyanne Chen; San Francisco Democratic Party; Saikat Chakrabarti.
> But the same tax break is also being given to financial institutions and corporate lenders when they acquire office towers, mega apartment complexes, and industrial buildings through foreclosure.

> It keeps the tax break for homeowners and small residential buildings, but makes sure that corporate lenders can't take advantage of this tax relief meant for struggling San Francisco families.

**Rebuttal to Proponent's Argument** (p. 148). Signers: Quentin L Kopp; San Francisco Taxpayers Association.
> What they fail to mention is that this money has no strings attached. It flows directly into the City's general fund, acting as a blank check for politicians ...

**Opponent's Argument** (p. 149). Signers: Judge Quentin L Kopp; San Francisco Taxpayers Association.
> This measure imposes heavy tax penalties on property foreclosures and deeds-in-lieu, severely crippling San Francisco's economic recovery and housing market by creating a punitive, unpredictable environment for commercial and residential lending.

**Rebuttal to Opponent's Argument** (p. 149). Signer: Supervisor Bilal Mahmood.
> Prop J doesn't touch families losing their homes. It only removes the tax break for corporate lenders that foreclose on office towers, mega apartment complexes, and industrial buildings.

## Legal text (pp. 241–242)

Text of the ordinance amending Article 12-C of the Business and Tax Regulations Code (Secs. 1101, 1108.2, 1111). First lines (p. 241):
> Proposition J
> Ordinance amending the Business and Tax Regulations Code to, beginning March 1, 2027, eliminate the real property transfer tax foreclosure exemption for the transfer of all properties other than the transfer of residential and mixed-use properties with fewer than five residential units, and to make other non-substantive clarifications to the real property transfer tax; and increasing the City's appropriations limit by the amount of real property transfer tax collected for four years from November 3, 2026.

Key operative text, Sec. 1108.2(c) (p. 241):
> Notwithstanding any other provision of this Article 12-C, the tax imposed with respect to any deed, instrument or writing to a beneficiary or mortgagee, which is taken from the mortgagor, trustor, or trustee, as a result of or in lieu of foreclosure, if such transfer is not subject to the exemption in subsection (a), shall be based on the fair market value of the interest or property conveyed ...
